# Validation against Target's own explanation

Source: Target FY2022 Form 10-K, filed 2023-03-08 (within the 2023-03-31 cutoff). The text was read in full; charts and images were not, including the gross margin chart on page 23.

**Status of this check:** consistency, not independent proof. This is management's own account, and the analysis was not fully blind (see the question and data-profile notes).

## Do the numbers match?

Revenue, cost of sales, SG&A, depreciation and operating income match the cleaned data exactly for FY2021 and FY2022 ($ millions).

Target's gross margin rate fell 28.3% → 23.6% (about 4.8 points unrounded). Our cost-of-goods rate rose 4.6 points. The gap is the denominator: Target divides by sales, we divide by total revenue.

## Hypotheses against the 10-K

| | What Target says | Result |
|---|---|---|
| H1 inventory and discounts | Higher clearance and promotional markdown rates, inventory impairments and other actions in Discretionary; costs of managing excess inventory | **Consistent.** Listed first, but the order is not stated to be a ranking |
| H2 freight | Higher "merchandise and freight costs", combined | **Consistent, still can't be sized** |
| H3 mix shift | "Favorable mix in the relative growth rates of higher and lower margin categories" | **Contradicted as a direct cause.** The fall in Discretionary demand is supported, as a reason for overstock |
| H4 supplier prices | Merchandise and freight costs rose, "partially offset" by retail price increases | **Partly supported.** "Didn't raise its own prices" was wrong: Target did |

Causes we didn't test as hypotheses, none sized by Target:
- **Supply chain costs:** distribution center pay and headcount, and new facilities. A candidate for the cost increase outside Q2.
- **Inventory shrink:** higher, as noted in the data-profile limitations.

## Corrections to earlier steps

1. **H3's size (analysis).** Census store sales showed the discretionary share falling 1.0 point. Target's own category sales (Note 3) show 3.3 points:

| Sales | FY2021 | FY2022 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discretionary (apparel, hardlines, home) | $56.8B | $54.8B | −3.4% |
| Frequency (beauty and essentials, food and beverage) | $47.6B | $52.5B | +10.3% |
| Discretionary share of sales | 54.3% | 51.0% | −3.3 points |

   The analysis's assumed 20-point margin gap would turn this into about 0.7 points instead of 0.2. Since Target says mix was favorable, that assumption looks doubtful and **neither figure should be used as a finding**.

2. **H4's wording (data profile).** Replace "Target didn't raise its own" with "price increases only partly offset higher costs".
3. **What cost of goods includes.** Note 4: product cost plus freight, markdowns, shrink, distribution center costs, outbound shipping and ship-from-store labor. A higher cost-of-goods rate doesn't by itself mean products cost more.
4. **SG&A (analysis).** "The FY2021 gain largely held" needs a caveat: lower incentive pay partly offset higher costs, including wages. Accrued wages and benefits fell $301M, which is consistent with this but doesn't measure it.

## What to question in Target's account

- **No sizes.** The text doesn't say how much came from markdowns, freight, supply chain or shrink.
- **The timing is incomplete.** Target says some inventory arrived early in the second half of 2022. Inventory was already up 43% in Q1 and 36% in Q2.
- **The build began in FY2021.** Inventory used $3.2B of cash that year and ended it up about 30%. Target says it had been ordering merchandise earlier. It doesn't connect that to the 2022 excess, and some of the build may have been a sensible response to strong demand and supply delays.
- **Forecasting.** The Risk Factors say Target has "not always been able to predict" rapid shifts in demand, resulting in "insufficient or excess inventory". Owned brands (about a third of sales) generally need longer lead times.

## Checked, and small

- **LIFO provision:** rose $99M ($33M → $132M), equal to about 2% of the $5.06B cost-rate effect.
- **Depreciation inside cost of sales:** rose about $17M.

## Peers

Walmart and Kohl's are both in Target's own 19-company peer group (Item 5).

## Analyst's conclusions (2026-11-23)

1. **H1 is the best explanation for the Q2 spike, and one of several for the full-year drop.**
2. **Both circumstances and decisions.** Demand shifted, but the FY2021 build and early ordering made Target more exposed. This is a judgment; the data doesn't prove it.
3. **Biggest surprise:** the inventory build started a year earlier, in FY2021.

## Carried into the recommendations

The question moves from "what went wrong in 2022?" to "what was decided in 2021, and what should change?"
